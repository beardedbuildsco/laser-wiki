# Ventilation and safety

Lasers create light energy, heat, fire risk, and airborne contaminants. A shop that skips ventilation and basic laser discipline eventually pays for it — in health, insurance, or a ruined machine.

This page is practical guidance for small shops. It is **not** a substitute for your machine’s safety documentation, eyewear specs, or local regulations.

!!! danger "Never leave a cutting job unattended"
    Materials catch fire. Stay present, keep an extinguisher rated for your materials within reach, and know how to e-stop and cut power.

## Know your laser class and enclosure

Machines are classified by accessible radiation hazard. Many hobby diodes are sold as open-frame Class 4 hazards when the beam is accessible; enclosed CO₂ cabinets with interlocks aim to reduce accessible beam exposure during normal use.

**Practical rules:**

- Understand whether your enclosure and interlocks are intact
- Use **wavelength-correct laser safety eyewear** whenever there is accessible beam risk (including alignment)
- Never bypass interlocks “just for a second”
- Keep unauthorized people and pets out of the beam path

## Eyes and skin

- Diffuse reflections can still be hazardous at laser wavelengths — do not stare into the work area without appropriate protection for your setup
- CO₂ and fiber/diode hazards differ by wavelength; **eyewear must match**
- Avoid accidental skin exposure to the direct beam

If you are unsure which OD (optical density) eyewear you need, stop and get the specification for your laser wavelength and power from a reputable safety supplier — guessing is not a plan.

## Fire

Common ignition sources: paper, thin wood, oils in hardwoods, acrylic flare-ups, dirty honeycomb with debris.

**Mitigations:**

- Air assist where appropriate
- Clean the bed so scrap scraps are not tinder
- Reasonable power/speed — “more power” is not always safer
- Water-cooled systems: confirm chiller flow interlocks work
- Know your extinguisher type for the materials you process; for electrical equipment, understand when to kill power first

## Fumes and particles

Cutting and engraving create smoke, ultrafine particles, and chemical byproducts that depend on the material. See [what you can laser](../materials/what-you-can-laser.md) for materials that are outright unsafe (especially chlorine-containing plastics).

**Ventilation approaches:**

1. **Exhaust outdoors** through sealed ducting with a fan sized for your enclosure — simplest mental model for many garages
2. **Filtration systems** designed for laser fume extraction — maintain filters on schedule; saturated filters stop protecting you
3. Hybrid setups — follow manufacturer guidance; do not improvise with household HVAC returns

!!! warning "Smell is a late warning"
    If you smell strong fumes in the operator area, your capture path is losing. Pause work and fix exhaust before “finishing one more job.”

## Electrical and cooling

- Use properly grounded outlets; avoid fragile DIY power strips for high-draw chillers and lasers
- Keep coolant clean on water-cooled tubes; algae and low flow kill tubes
- Route cables so the gantry cannot chafe through insulation

## Workplace habits that prevent accidents

| Habit | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-flight checklist (exhaust on, focus set, frame checked) | Catches dumb mistakes |
| Material ID before cutting | Avoids PVC disasters |
| Lid closed / interlocks respected | Beam containment |
| No alcohol/solvent wipe-downs while beam can fire | Flammable vapors |
| Training for anyone who “just wants to try it” | Lasers are not toys |

## Emergency basics

1. E-stop / stop in LightBurn
2. Kill laser power if fire continues
3. Extinguish only if safe to approach
4. Ventilate after, then inspect optics and wiring before the next run
5. Document near-misses so procedures improve
